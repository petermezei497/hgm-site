Once upon a time, beyond the vast oceans of existential anxiety, there lived a human community that discovered how to awaken a long-forgotten part of human consciousness.

In this world, the economy was not driven by cold spreadsheets or endless pressure for growth, but by something entirely different: the energy of shared joy.

They called it: Joyland.

The people of Joyland discovered something remarkable: the safer the human soul felt, the more curious, creative, and cooperative people became.

That is why children were included in community life from a very early age — not so they could become “useful members of society” as quickly as possible, but so they could discover what they truly shined at.

They paid attention to who loved telling stories, who instinctively comforted others, who could lose themselves in an idea for hours, and who came alive while creating something.

The people of Joyland did not go to work merely to survive, but to create and unfold themselves.

They collaborated in small communities where people worked when they felt most alive. They did not organise life around the clock, but around human rhythm.

A dignified living was guaranteed to everyone, and the profit created together was shared naturally amongst those who contributed to it.

Alongside four days of shared creation, everyone had one day free from obligations.

Many simply called it “the day of silence”.
On those days, people walked in forests, played, made music, watched the wind moving through the trees, or simply allowed themselves to be bored.

And strangely enough, the best ideas often emerged from that silence.
New inventions.  
Community projects.  
Childhood dreams.

When someone discovered a dream within themselves, the community did not laugh at it. They gathered around it instead.
They listened.  
They asked questions.  
They helped shape the idea further.
And if the dream moved the hearts of others too, they helped bring it to life together.  

The elderly never disappeared from community life either.
As their physical strength declined, they increasingly became mentors, storytellers, guides, and quiet presences beside those who needed companionship.

In Joyland, people understood something important:
a person’s value did not come from how much they produced,  
but from how they connected with others.

So they looked after one another.  
Helped one another.  
And saw their differences not as threats, but as shared sources of strength.  

And whenever someone asked them how they had managed to create such a world, they simply smiled:
“We realised that the human soul was never designed for endless exploitation.”

Maybe this is not a utopia of the distant future.

Maybe it is simply a forgotten memory of what humanity has always been capable of becoming.

Because every world is first born as a story. ❤️
